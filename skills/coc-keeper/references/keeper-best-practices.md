## Missing but important rule habits

### 1. Distinguish obvious clues from hidden clues

- `显明线索`: do not require a roll if the story needs the clue to move
- `隐秘线索`: use perception-style rolls only for optional or advantageous detail
- if a hidden clue is missed, the player should feel the tension of having missed something

This is one of the cleanest ways to keep investigations moving without flattening suspense.

### 2. Use Inspiration checks when the table is stuck

- if players are stalled, a `灵感检定` can restore momentum
- the clue still appears whether the check succeeds or fails
- success means the clue arrives more cleanly
- failure means the clue arrives inside a worse situation or under pressure

This is stronger than repeatedly fishing for the same clue with normal checks.

### 3. Avoid repeated rolls for the same intent

- one meaningful roll should usually resolve one meaningful objective
- if the same objective is attempted again, consider `孤注一骰`
- do not ask for multiple nearly identical rolls when one harder roll would do

### 4. Let failure mean cost, not absence

- failure should cost time, position, safety, certainty, or resources
- total dead ends should be rare
- reserve truly ugly outcomes for `孤注一骰` failure, fumbles, or situations already established as lethal

### 5. Ask for intent before calling the roll

- first ask what the investigator is actually trying to achieve
- then set the target, the skill, and the difficulty
- after the roll, narrate toward that declared intent

This keeps checks fiction-first instead of rule-first.

## Keeper-facing best practices

### 6. Give players room to narrate their success

- on a successful check, invite the player to describe how they achieve the result
- keep authority over consequences and new facts that go beyond the declared goal
- if the player’s narration extends the outcome too far, trim or clarify rather than shutting it down flatly

### 7. Use “yes, and” or “yes, but”

- when the player proposes an unexpected but workable angle, try to integrate it
- avoid bluntly forcing them back to your planned path
- preserve their agency while protecting the scenario’s tension

### 8. Keep official institutions human, not cartoonish

- police, judges, employers, and local officials can help, obstruct, or misunderstand
- do not default to making every authority figure useless or corrupt
- a more mixed portrayal makes the world feel worth saving

### 9. Reuse NPCs and tie them to player backgrounds

- recurring NPCs create continuity
- background hooks matter more than generic exposition
- when possible, connect clues or danger to what the investigator already cares about

### 10. Twist background entries after madness or trauma

- a failed SAN sequence can distort a bond, belief, treasured item, or personal motivation
- prefer corruption of an existing background detail over inventing something unrelated

## Tactical scene handling

### 11. Give warnings before lethal commitment

- before a clearly deadly choice, provide up to three warning opportunities when possible
- warnings can come from rumor, visible evidence, terrified NPC testimony, or environmental signs
- this preserves fairness without draining fear

### 12. Separate combat resolution from combat framing

- some scenes only need a single action roll and narration
- use full round structure when multiple actors, timing, and positional changes matter
- for stealth ambushes, remember surprise can mean automatic success or a bonus, depending on notice rolls

### 13. Use hidden rolls deliberately

- dark or hidden rolls are useful for NPC reactions, some psychology uncertainty, and situations where knowing the result would distort player behavior
- do not overuse them; players should still roll often

### 14. Psychology is uncertainty, not always truth

- on a failed `心理学` check, avoid always giving the opposite of the truth
- uncertainty is often stronger than a clean false answer

## Tone and horror

### 15. Horror works better through traces before full reveal

- smell, damage, rearranged objects, strange habits, body fragments, impossible behavior
- reveal the entity through evidence and effects before presenting the whole creature
- `less is more` applies especially well to mythos entities

### 16. Intelligent mythos entities should feel alien, not just aggressive

- they can experiment, ritualize, collect, reshape, or use humans indirectly
- not every scene with a mythos being should collapse into immediate combat

## Endings and session close

### 17. End scenes with a hook or a consequence

- each major scene should either change the world, sharpen a threat, or reveal a new line forward
- when pausing, leave a clear next hook

### 18. Use short epilogues

- after a session or scenario, give each surviving investigator a brief aftermath
- this creates emotional closure and makes victories or damage feel real
